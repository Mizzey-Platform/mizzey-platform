## 1. Purpose of this document

This is an invitation to submit a proposal for the complete visual design of a new premium e-commerce business. The engagement covers the creation of a full brand identity from zero, and the design of the entire website user interface.

This document is written to be self-contained. It specifies every deliverable, constraint, format and requirement needed to prepare a complete and accurate quotation without further clarification.

Please read it in full before preparing a proposal. Section 14 sets out what the proposal should contain.

## 2. About Mizzey

Mizzey is a new online store in Egypt. It sells a carefully selected and continuously refreshed range of products, including trending items, gadgets that simplify daily life, premium and imported goods, gifts, travel essentials, smart home products, and other high value picks.

The business currently has a name and a domain, and nothing else. There is no logo, no colour palette, no typography, no visual identity and no existing brand assets of any kind. Everything is created new in this engagement.

The website is built on WordPress with WooCommerce as the commerce platform, selected by the developer. It launches multilingual: **English as the primary and default storefront language, and Arabic fully supported at launch with complete right to left layouts.**

### 2.1 Positioning

**Premium. Curated. Trend driven. Deliberately not a discount store.**

The experience should feel selective and confident, closer to a well edited retailer that happens to sell online than to a marketplace competing on price.

This rules out aggressive red sale badges, countdown timer urgency, cluttered high density grids, and discount led visual language.

It calls for generous spacing, restrained colour, considered typography and quiet confidence.

### 2.2 Audience

Egyptian Class A and upper middle class shoppers who are willing to pay more for quality, convenience and trust. They value authenticity, delivery speed, easy returns and an organised buying experience. They are time poor, and want good products found for them rather than hunting for them.

### 2.3 Category neutrality, a defining constraint

The design must not be tied to any single product category.

Mizzey sells across many unrelated categories, and that range will keep expanding. Today it may be electronics, home goods, travel accessories and gifts. In a year it may include categories nobody has considered yet.

This means the visual identity and the interface must work equally well for a phone accessory, a kitchen tool, a suitcase, a perfume, a piece of jewellery or a smart home device.

In practice:

* The logo must not depict or allude to any specific product type
* Iconography must remain generic and category neutral
* Photography treatment and card layouts must flatter varied product shapes, whether tall, wide, small or irregular
* Colour and typography must not read as belonging to one sector
* Section headings and layout patterns must accommodate any category without redesign
* Packaging must suit any product type placed inside it

The identity is a premium container, not a themed shop.

## 3. Non negotiable requirements

### 3.1 Originality

All delivered work must be entirely original and created specifically for this project. The logo in particular must be a unique, bespoke design.

The following are not acceptable: purchased or modified stock logos, logo generator output, templates, marketplace assets, previously delivered work reused from another client, or designs derived from existing brands.

The designer is asked to warrant in writing that all delivered work is original, does not infringe any third party rights, and has not been licensed or delivered to any other party.

### 3.2 Figma

All brand identity and website design work is produced in Figma, and delivered as editable files with editor access shared with the client.

### 3.3 English is the canonical design direction, with full Arabic RTL support

Mizzey is a multilingual English and Arabic storefront. **English is the primary and default storefront language, and English is the canonical design source.** Arabic is the second fully supported storefront language, delivered in full at launch as the right to left version of the same approved experience.

**The English version is the master design.** In English the designer establishes the visual hierarchy, the layout, the component system, the grids, the page composition, the navigation, the typography, component sizing, responsive behaviour, states, interactions and the visual system as a whole.

**The Arabic version is then designed as the RTL version of that same system.** It is not a separate aesthetic concept and not a second brand. It is the Arabic right to left implementation of the Mizzey brand and experience, and it must include:

* Complete right to left mirroring wherever mirroring is appropriate
* Right aligned layout logic
* Arabic typography
* Right to left navigation patterns
* Directional icon handling, and correct arrows, carousels and breadcrumbs
* Correct form alignment, and correct product card behaviour
* Arabic text expansion and contraction handling, and Arabic content density
* Responsive right to left layouts

Both directions must work from the same component set. Layouts must mirror cleanly, and components must tolerate direction change without breaking.

**Arabic is not a later phase, not partial, not optional and not a reduced version.** It launches with the store.

### 3.4 Typography in both scripts, handled properly

English typography is defined first, since English is the canonical design. The Arabic pairing is then chosen to sit within the same premium brand rather than beside it.

**English typography.** A complete type scale with weights, sizes, line heights and letter spacing defined for every style, established on the English screens and forming the reference hierarchy for the system.

**Arabic typography.** The Arabic face must visually belong to the same premium brand, remain highly readable on mobile, support the required Arabic glyphs, work cleanly at interface sizes, and hold an appropriate weight hierarchy.

Identical typographic metrics between the two scripts are neither required nor wanted. **Aim for equivalent visual hierarchy, not identical character dimensions.**

* Arabic requires greater line height than Latin, and must be tested at realistic content lengths
* Arabic text runs roughly 20 to 30 percent longer than English for equivalent meaning, so buttons, labels and navigation sized on the English design must absorb this without truncation
* The typeface must have genuine Arabic support across multiple weights. IBM Plex Sans Arabic, Cairo, Tajawal and Almarai are acceptable starting points, and the designer should propose what best fits the positioning
* Either Arabic Indic numerals or Latin numerals should be recommended and applied consistently, with the reasoning stated
* Mixed content is normal, since Arabic descriptions will contain English brand and product names. This must be designed for deliberately

### 3.5 Mobile first, in both directions

Mobile is designed first, then desktop, since most traffic will be mobile.

Breakpoints: 360, 768, 1024 and 1440.

**Both directions must be validated on mobile: English LTR and Arabic RTL.** A layout that resolves in English on a small screen does not automatically resolve in Arabic, and right to left responsive validation is part of the expected handoff rather than something discovered during build.

### 3.6 A design system, not a collection of screens

The deliverable is a system of reusable, documented components with named tokens, not individually crafted pages.

### 3.7 Built for WooCommerce

The store runs on WordPress with WooCommerce. Standard, proven commerce interaction patterns are required. The visual identity should be distinctive, but the interaction patterns should be familiar. Novel checkout flows or non standard cart behaviour are not wanted.

The developer also uses an in-house framework layer, Corex, above WooCommerce for custom project functionality. It changes nothing about the design work and is mentioned only so the technical context is complete.

### 3.8 Extensibility

The system must allow the client team to build new pages, sections and campaigns in future without further design input. This requirement is expanded in section 5.2 and is central to the engagement.

### 12.2 Revisions

A minimum of two consolidated revision rounds per stage should be included in the quoted price. Proposals may offer more, and should say so clearly.

### 12.3 What the proposal should specify

Design projects more often run into difficulty over revisions than over the original work, so this section is treated as a material part of the proposal.

Please address each of the following.

**Number of rounds.** How many revision rounds are included at each of the six stages. The logo stage and the website screens stage typically attract the most feedback, so please state these explicitly rather than giving a single blanket figure.

**Definition of a round.** What counts as one revision round. For example, whether a round is one consolidated set of written comments, and how a follow up question or a small correction after a round is treated.

**Revision versus new work.** Where the line is drawn between adjusting existing work and creating new work. For example, changing a colour or adjusting spacing, compared with restructuring a screen layout or replacing a component.

**Turnaround.** The expected turnaround time for each revision round, at each stage.

**Feedback format.** How feedback should be submitted, whether as Figma comments, a written document, or another method, and any requirements around consolidating comments before a round begins.

**Beyond the included rounds.** What happens once the included rounds are used, the rate for additional rounds, and how this would be raised before the work is done rather than after.

**Full concept rejection.** What happens if none of the three logo concepts is accepted. Whether a further set of concepts is included, and if not, what it would cost. It is better for both sides that this is agreed in advance rather than negotiated at the time.

**Direction change after approval.** What happens if the client requests a change to something already approved in writing at an earlier stage.

**Approval mechanism.** How stage approval is recorded and confirmed.

### 12.4 Client commitments

In return, the client commits to consolidating all feedback into a single set per round, routing all feedback through one named person with approval authority, and confirming stage approval in writing before the next stage begins.

**Existing assets.** None. No logo, colours, typography, imagery or guidelines exist. The name and the domain are the only fixed elements.

**Name origin.** Mizzey is a coined name with no prescribed meaning. The designer is free to interpret it.

**Language of delivery.** **English is the canonical design language and every screen is presented in English first.** The Arabic RTL version is then delivered for the core storefront templates listed in section 7, as the right to left localisation of the approved English design. Where the Arabic version of a minor state or variant follows directly from documented direction rules rather than a distinct layout, that is acceptable, provided the rules are written down.

**Dark mode.** Not required for launch. Tokens or structures that would ease its later addition are welcome but not mandatory.

**Accessibility.** WCAG 2.1 Level AA for colour contrast, focus indication and touch target sizing.

**Animation.** Motion principles and token values are required. Detailed animation specification or interactive prototyping is not required, and should be priced separately if included.

**Icon set.** A custom set is expected rather than an unmodified open source library, to maintain category neutrality and brand consistency.

**Product imagery for mockups.** Placeholder product imagery may be used for presentation purposes. Real photography is supplied by the client at a later stage.

**Number of product categories.** Approximately six to ten top level categories at launch, expanding over time. The design should not assume a fixed number.

**Screen count.** Thirty five screens are required, listed in section 7 and numbered 1 to 34 with an additional screen 15a, each at desktop and mobile, plus the overlays in section 7.5. Each is designed in English as the canonical version, and the core storefront templates additionally require an Arabic RTL version, as set out in section 7. Three further screens, O1 to O3 in section 7.6, are optional and priced separately. **Please state clearly in the proposal how the Arabic RTL versions are counted and priced**, since this is the single most common source of disagreement on a bilingual engagement.

**Figma seats.** The client provides the Figma project and the seats required.

**Packaging production.** The client arranges printing and production. The designer supplies print ready artwork only, and is not responsible for print quality or supplier coordination.

**Content responsibility.** All product content, marketing copy and legal text is written by the client.

**Concurrent development.** Website development runs in parallel with design, so a phased delivery schedule is preferred over a single final handover.
